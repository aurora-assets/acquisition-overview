Aurora Primitive Derivation Method (APDM)
The generative engine behind all Aurora operators
Aurora’s primitives are not vocabulary items.
They are semantic actuators — structured operators that determine how meaning forms, moves, binds, and dissolves between agents.
APDM defines a repeatable method for constructing these operators.
Any primitive in Aurora — WE, I, YOU, WITH, THROUGH, BELONGING, ANY — is produced by the same underlying procedure.
This is what gives the architecture its power:
finite rules → infinite primitives.

1. Identify the Semantic Function
Every primitive in Aurora begins with a function, not a word.
Ask:
What transformation between agents or concepts does this operator perform?
Examples:
combine perspectives
isolate perspectives
switch roles
create boundaries
dissolve boundaries
bind two agents to a shared domain
This isolates the operator’s core purpose.

2. Define the Invariants
A primitive is only valid if certain conditions are always true.
These are its invariants — the structural rules that cannot break.
For WE, examples included:
≥ 2 roles
shared domain
speaker included
mutual interpretability above threshold
Every primitive has its own invariant set.
These determine how the operator behaves regardless of context.

3. Establish Boundary Formation
Aurora primitives are boundary-aware.
Define:
How does this primitive carve the world into inside vs. outside?
A boundary may separate:
roles
domains
states
perspectives
semantic frames
conceptual intervals
Every primitive forms a unique topological boundary.
This boundary is the engine of meaning.

4. Define the Activation Conditions
When does the primitive fire?
What must be present for the operator to instantiate?
Examples:
role alignment
domain overlap
perspective contact
conceptual adjacency
threshold similarity
Activation conditions determine the “entry point” of the operator.

5. Specify the Semantic Transformation
This is the heart of the primitive:
What does the operator do once active?
Does it:
merge perspectives?
shift alignment?
create a composite agent?
transfer salience?
orient attention?
redistribute meaning-weight?
This is the internal mechanism of the primitive.

6. Define the Collapse Rule
Every Aurora primitive has conditions under which it stops working.
The collapse rule is the “exit condition,” and it matters just as much as activation.
Examples:
domain dissolved
boundary breaks
role misalignment
interpretability falls below threshold
Collapse rules give primitives lifecycles.
LLMs do not have semantic lifecycles — Aurora does.

7. Identify the Automatic Complement
Aurora primitives generate their own negations.
Once invariants and collapse are defined, the complement emerges automatically.
For WE:
NOT-WE = any state where WE invariants do not hold.
This creates a full semantic field without writing new rules.

8. Verify Generalisability
A primitive is valid if:
its invariants are coherent,
its activation is precise,
its collapse is deterministic,
its complement is automatic,
and it can appear in infinite contexts without contradiction.
This confirms the primitive is robust enough for computation.

Result
Using APDM, any Aurora primitive can be constructed with:
precision
consistency
boundary-awareness
implementability
and a clear lifecycle
This method is the core of the Aurora architecture.
It demonstrates that the system is scalable, systematic, and commercially viable.
